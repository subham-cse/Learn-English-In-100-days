# Day 40 : Relative Clauses

## 📚 Overview
A **Relative Clause** (also called an *adjective clause*) connects two ideas by providing essential identification or extra background information about a preceding noun. Choosing the correct relative pronoun (**who, whom, which, that, whose**) and determining whether a clause is **defining** (restrictive) or **non-defining** (non-restrictive) controls both punctuation (commas) and sentence meaning.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Select the appropriate relative pronoun based on person, thing, possession, or grammatical role.
- Differentiate between defining and non-defining relative clauses in meaning and punctuation.
- Apply comma rules correctly (defining = no commas; non-defining = separated by commas).
- Recognize when a relative pronoun can be omitted (object relative clauses).
- Avoid common errors, such as using "that" in non-defining clauses or repeating redundant object pronouns.

---

## 📖 Theoretical Breakdown

### 1. Relative Pronouns and Their Functions

| Pronoun | Refers To | Grammatical Role | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **who** | People | Subject / Object | *The engineer **who** designed the algorithm won an award.* |
| **whom** | People | Object *(formal)* | *The candidate **whom** we interviewed yesterday was impressive.* |
| **which** | Animals, Things, Ideas | Subject / Object | *The laptop **which** was sitting on the desk crashed.* |
| **that** | People, Animals, Things | Subject / Object *(Defining only)* | *The laptop **that** I purchased yesterday runs smoothly.* |
| **whose** | Possession (People/Things) | Possessive Determiner | *The author **whose** novel won the Booker Prize gave a talk.* |
| **where / when** | Places / Times | Relative Adverbs | *The city **where** we held the conference was vibrant.* |

---

### 2. Defining vs. Non-Defining Relative Clauses

Understanding this distinction is one of the most critical punctuation and meaning milestones in English grammar:

#### A. Defining Relative Clauses (Restrictive)
* **Purpose:** Provides **essential information** needed to identify which specific person or thing is being discussed. Without it, the sentence is incomplete or ambiguous.
* **Punctuation:** **NO commas**.
* **Pronoun Choice:** Can use `who`, `which`, or `that`.
* *Example:*
  - *Students **who work consistently** achieve excellent marks.*  
    *(Meaning: Only those specific students who work consistently, not all students).*
  - *The company **that** manufactured this device has closed.*

#### B. Non-Defining Relative Clauses (Non-Restrictive)
* **Purpose:** Adds **supplementary, non-essential information**. If removed, the core identity of the noun remains clear and unchanged.
* **Punctuation:** **Enclosed in commas** (or followed by a comma if at the end of the sentence).
* **Pronoun Restrictions:** **CANNOT use "that"**. Must use `who`, `which`, `whom`, or `whose`.
* *Example:*
  - *Albert Einstein, **who was born in Ulm**, formulated the theory of relativity.*  
    *(We already know who Albert Einstein is; the birthplace is extra biographical context).*
  - *Our London branch, **which opened in 2018**, achieved record revenue this quarter.*

#### ⚡ The "Meaning Pivot" with Commas:
- *The employees who attended the training received a certificate.*  
  $\rightarrow$ Only the specific subset of employees who attended got certificates.
- *The employees, who attended the training, received a certificate.*  
  $\rightarrow$ All employees attended the training, and all received certificates.

---

### 3. Omitting the Relative Pronoun (Object Clauses)
In **defining relative clauses**, the relative pronoun (*who, which, that*) can be omitted **if and only if** it functions as the **object** of the relative clause verb (meaning a new subject follows):

* **Subject Role (CANNOT OMIT):**
  - *The technician [who repaired the machine] is here.* $\rightarrow$ (*who* is the subject of *repaired*; cannot drop *who*).
* **Object Role (CAN OMIT):**
  - *The book [that you recommended] was fascinating.* $\rightarrow$ *The book [you recommended] was fascinating.* (*you* is the subject of *recommended*).
  - *The colleague [whom we hired] starts tomorrow.* $\rightarrow$ *The colleague [we hired] starts tomorrow.*

---

### 4. Prepositions in Relative Clauses
* **Formal / Academic (Fronted):** The preposition precedes the pronoun (*whom* or *which* only).
  - *The executive **to whom** the email was addressed responded promptly.*
  - *The project **on which** we collaborated was approved.*
* **Informal / Conversational (Stranded):** The preposition rests at the end of the clause.
  - *The executive **that** the email was addressed **to** responded promptly.*

---

### 5. Common Errors to Avoid
1. **Using "that" in non-defining clauses:**
   - *Incorrect:* Apple Inc., that is based in Cupertino, reported earnings.
   - *Correct:* Apple Inc., **which** is based in Cupertino, reported earnings.
2. **Double Pronoun / Redundant Resumption:**
   - *Incorrect:* The presentation that I prepared **it** yesterday went well.
   - *Correct:* The presentation that I prepared yesterday went well. *(Remove the redundant "it")*.

## 💬 Conversational Dialogue

**Context:** Clara and Simon are discussing vendors and partners for their annual technology summit.

* **Clara:** "Simon, do you recall the cybersecurity specialist **whose** keynote presentation went viral last year?"
* **Simon:** "Yes, Dr. Sophia Vance! She is the expert **who** founded CyberGuard, correct?"
* **Clara:** "Exactly. She just released a new book **that** focuses on quantum encryption."
* **Simon:** "Fascinating. Should we invite her keynote agency, **which** is based in Boston?"
* **Clara:** "Yes, please reach out to her agent. And by the way, the contract **(that)** you drafted this morning looks flawless."
* **Simon:** "Great! Notice how cleanly we can omit 'that' when it is the object of the verb 'drafted'."
* **Clara:** "A hallmark of fluent, natural English!"

## ✍️ Practice Exercises

### Exercise A: Combine Sentences Using Relative Clauses
Combine the two sentences into a single sentence using an appropriate relative pronoun. Add commas if the clause is non-defining.
1. The architect designed the city museum. She received an international accolade.
2. The laptop was stolen from the office. It contained proprietary client data.
3. Leonardo da Vinci painted the Mona Lisa. He was also a pioneer of anatomical sketches.
4. I interviewed a researcher. His breakthrough publication transformed nanotechnology.

### Exercise B: Identify and Fix Punctuation / Pronoun Errors
5. The Amazon River that is the second longest river in the world flows through South America.
6. The software which I downloaded it yesterday crashed twice.
7. Is this the hotel where you stayed during your holiday? *(Determine if correct or incorrect)*

---

## 🔑 Self-Check Answer Key

### Exercise A:
1. **The architect who designed the city museum received an international accolade.** *(Defining; no commas).*
2. **The laptop that (or which) was stolen from the office contained proprietary client data.** *(Defining; no commas).*
3. **Leonardo da Vinci, who painted the Mona Lisa, was also a pioneer of anatomical sketches.** *(Non-defining; commas required; "that" cannot be used).*
4. **I interviewed a researcher whose breakthrough publication transformed nanotechnology.** *(Possessive relative pronoun "whose").*

### Exercise B:
5. **Error:** Missing commas and incorrect use of "that" for a unique noun.  
   $\rightarrow$ **Correction:** The Amazon River, **which** is the second longest river in the world, flows through South America.
6. **Error:** Redundant pronoun "it".  
   $\rightarrow$ **Correction:** The software which (or that) I downloaded yesterday crashed twice. *(Or omit the pronoun: "The software I downloaded yesterday crashed twice").*
7. **Correct:** Uses the relative adverb "where" properly without redundant prepositions.

## 💡 Practical Tip for Daily Practice
> **The Finger Test for Commas:** Put your finger over the relative clause and read the sentence without it.
> - If the main noun loses its identity (e.g. *"The man was arrested"* — which man?), the clause is **defining** $\rightarrow$ **NO commas**, "that" is allowed.
> - If the noun is already fully identified (e.g. *"My father,"* or *"Tokyo,"*), the clause is **non-defining** $\rightarrow$ **COMMAS required**, never use "that".
